The problem

A trade doesn’t end when the order is created.

One agricultural trade can pass through approvals, procurement, several truck deliveries, quality and weight differences, adjustments, payments to several parties, tax and reconciliation. Six teams touch the same transaction, and each needs a different slice of it at a different moment.

Treated as separate screens, every handoff forces someone to rebuild the trade’s history by hand: which deliveries arrived, what changed and why, what is still owed, and whether the trade can be closed.

One transaction, many records

Sales orders, purchase orders, deliveries, payments and adjustments can’t be designed in isolation. One purchase order can feed many deliveries.

The happy path isn’t the product

Partial deliveries, unloading differences and payment adjustments are everyday work, not edge cases.

Every role decides differently

Procurement, sales, risk and finance need different visibility, permissions and next actions on the same record.

Design questionHow might we make a multi-stage trade readable, actionable and auditable for every role, without anyone reconstructing its history by hand?

Decision 01 · Lists

Organise work by the next action, not by the record

People working an operations list don’t open it to browse. They come to clear work. So instead of one long list of records, work is grouped by what needs to happen next, and each row offers only the action its current state allows.

Groups follow handoffs

Each group marks a point where work moves from one team to the next, so a person’s day maps to the groups they own.

Status sits beside the action

An action appears only when the state allows it, so nobody hunts for a button that isn’t valid yet.

Search speaks operations

Search by the identifiers people actually quote on a call, not by internal database fields.

Decision 02 · Completion

Make “complete” mean something, and show what it changes

Closing an order too early leaves money unsettled; closing it too late leaves records open to edits nobody should make. I designed completion as a visible gate: the system checks for outstanding work, explains anything that blocks it, and tells the user what becomes read-only before they commit.

Decision 03 · Settlement

Explain every rupee, and keep each party’s balance apart

An adjustment is never just a negative number. Finance needs to see what it is, who raised it and what remains. Each counterparty, and tax, settles on its own timeline, so each gets its own balance and its own payment flow instead of one blended total.

Show the calculation

Payable amounts read top to bottom, from agreed value to what’s pending, so nobody has to rebuild the maths.

Make reasons auditable

Each adjustment links back to the event and person behind it.

State in colour and words

Statuses use one consistent scheme: green settled, amber needs attention, red stopped, always with a text label.

Decision 04 · Across devices

Same transaction, different depth on mobile

On FarMart PRO, a trade keeps the same identity and the same money structure as on the OS, cut down to what someone needs on site: what’s pending, and the next action they’re allowed to take. An adjustment on a phone stays traceable to a person and a reason.

Shared vocabulary

The same labels and the same order on both products, so a call between field and finance needs no translation.

Attributed adjustments

Every adjustment shows who raised it and why, on the phone as well as the desktop.

Consistent state

When a trade’s state changes on the OS, the phone reflects it, so it never offers an action the system will refuse.

Pattern

One page anatomy for every record

Dense operational data stays readable when every record page answers questions in the same order. I designed detail pages toward this hierarchy, supported by a small set of reusable components: status badges, dense tables with row actions, detail panels for linked records, confirmation dialogs and permission-aware read-only states.

Permissions

Permissions designed action by action

Rather than broad admin tiers, the design treats each sensitive action as its own permission, tied to the state of the record. The UI makes those rules visible instead of letting people discover them through errors.

Explain before blocking

Unavailable actions say why and who can take them, instead of silently disappearing.

Read-only is a designed state

Locked records keep their full detail and history, styled so nobody mistakes them for broken.

Exceptions leave a trail

Any override asks for a written reason that stays visible on the record.

Trade-offs

What I had to balance

Complexityvsclarity

Experts want every relationship; occasional users get lost. Decision-relevant information comes first, and the rest sits one click deeper.

Flexibilityvscontrol

Operations need edits and exceptions; finance needs locks and audit trails. A controlled exception path keeps control from blocking real work.

Local taskvswhole trade

A delivery operator needs the next action; an order reviewer needs the rollup across every delivery. The same record serves both views.

Speedvsconfidence

Quick actions help daily work, but completion and payments get explicit checks and clear consequence messages.

Measuring next

I’m only publishing numbers I can back up. These are the measures I’d use to quantify the impact properly:

  • Time from delivery to order completion, before and after
  • Approval turnaround and exception-resolution time
  • Payment reconciliation effort and error rate
  • Adoption by role, and support tickets per workflow

What I learned

The hardest work is invisible

An enterprise system isn’t a set of attractive screens. The real work is ownership, linked objects, exceptions and traceable money. The strongest artifact I made was a model that lets anyone, on any screen, answer five questions:

What is happening?

Why is it in this state?

Who needs to act next?

What else will this action affect?

How will we know it’s complete?
